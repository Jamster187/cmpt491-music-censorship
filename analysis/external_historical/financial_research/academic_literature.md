# Academic evidence

Checked 2026-10-28. Findings below belong to the cited authors, not to our dataset. The summaries distinguish lyric text, acoustic positivity, actual listening and chart selection. Those are different measurements. Neither a published association nor a reported predictive result establishes causality or expected performance in our study.

**1. Harold M. Zullow (1991), “Pessimistic rumination in popular songs and newsmagazines predict economic recession via decreased consumer optimism and spending.”** *Journal of Economic Psychology* 12(3), 501–526; peer reviewed. [Publisher/DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-4870(91)90029-S).
Dataset: annual US top-40 lyrics, 1955–1989, Time captions, consumer surveys and macroeconomic outcomes. Method: content coding and simultaneous equations estimated with two-stage least squares. Reported finding: pessimistic rumination preceded reduced optimism and spending. Limits: short annual sample, historical measurement and identification assumptions; this is not a demonstrated modern forecasting system. Relevance: direct precedent for sentiment, consumer expectations and spending, and a reason to separate timing from causal claims. Publisher abstract reviewed; full methodological replication not performed.

**2. Terry F. Pettijohn II and Donald F. Sacco Jr. (2009), “The Language of Lyrics: An Analysis of Popular Billboard Songs Across Conditions of Social and Economic Threat.”** *Journal of Language and Social Psychology* 28(3), 297–311; peer reviewed. [Publisher](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X09335259).
Dataset: one annual Billboard number-one song, 1955–2003. Method: listener ratings, LIWC features and associations with social/economic conditions. Reported finding: more threatening periods coincided with more comforting/meaningful themes and changes in linguistic structure. Limits: only one song per year, subjective coding and aggregate confounding. Relevance: supports testing comfort versus negative-expression hypotheses separately rather than equating hardship with uniformly negative music. Abstract and accessible methodological notes reviewed.

**3. Terry F. Pettijohn II, Jason T. Eastman and Keith G. Richard (2012), “And the Beat Goes On: Popular Billboard Song Beats Per Minute and Key Signatures Vary with Social and Economic Conditions.”** *Current Psychology* 31, 313–317; peer reviewed. [Publisher](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-012-9149-y).
Dataset: popular Billboard songs, 1955–2008. Method: tempo/key comparisons with environmental social/economic indicators. Reported finding: faster songs and common keys were more prominent in favorable conditions. Limits: acoustic characteristics are not lyrical sentiment; aggregate observational relationships and limited chart sampling. Relevance: complementary audio and lyric channels should not be treated as interchangeable. Publisher abstract reviewed; inaccessible full methods are not claimed verified.

**4. Philip Maymin (2012), “Music and the Market: Song and Stock Volatility.”** *North American Journal of Economics and Finance* 23(1), 70–85; peer reviewed. [Author manuscript](https://philipmaymin.com/Maymin-Music-Market.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.najef.2011.11.004).
Dataset: annual Billboard song beat variance and S&P 500 return volatility, 1958–2007. Method: annual contemporaneous/lagged association and a proposed volatility strategy. Reported finding: negative association between musical beat variance and market volatility. Limits: audio variability rather than lyrics, small annual sample, strategy-selection and historical-data concerns. Relevance: motivates realized-volatility comparisons but supplies no evidence that our lyric classifiers predict returns. Author abstract/manuscript reviewed; strategy not reproduced.

**5. Jason T. Eastman and Terry F. Pettijohn II (2015), “Gone Country: An Investigation of Billboard Country Songs of the Year Across Social and Economic Conditions in the USA.”** *Psychology of Popular Media Culture* 4(2), 155–171; peer reviewed. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/ppm0000019), [author-hosted paper](https://www.tpettijohn.net/academic/Eastman%20%26%20Pettijohn%20%282015%29%20-%20Gone%20Country.pdf).
Dataset: annual leading country songs, 1946–2008. Method: coded song/performer characteristics compared with economic/social conditions. Reported finding: favorable-sounding country selections in hard times, unlike some aggregate-pop results. Limits: genre-specific annual winners and intertwined performer/content variables. Relevance: genre heterogeneity is substantive, not just a control variable. Primary indexed author text verified; direct full-PDF fetch timed out, so detailed estimates remain unreviewed.

**6. Lin Qiu, Sarah Hian May Chan, Kenichi Ito and Joyce Yan Ting Sam (2020 online), “Unemployment Rate Predicts Anger in Popular Music Lyrics: Evidence From Top 10 Songs in the United States and Germany From 1980 to 2017.”** *Psychology of Popular Media*; peer reviewed. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/ppm0000282), [author paper](https://personal.ntu.edu.sg/linqiu/publications/unemployment%20and%20anger%20in%20lyrics.pdf).
Dataset: annual top-ten songs in two countries, 1980–2017. Method: LIWC anger/anxiety/sadness and regressions with macroeconomic controls. Reported finding: unemployment related to anger, but not anxiety or sadness. Limits: lexicon validity, small annual time series, language differences and popular-song selection. Relevance: emotion dimensions may behave differently; sentiment valence alone can hide that distinction. Primary author/publisher text reviewed; original estimates not rerun.

**7. Juan de Lucio and Marco Palomeque (2023; online 2022), “Music preferences as an instrument of emotional self-regulation along the business cycle.”** *Journal of Cultural Economics* 47, 181–204; peer reviewed. [Open article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10824-022-09454-7).
Dataset: weekly Hot 100, 1958–2019, collected lyrics, VADER and Spotify acoustic valence; macroeconomic variables. Method: aggregate sentiment regressions and robustness comparisons. Reported finding: worse macroeconomic conditions, notably unemployment, accompanied more positive music preferences. Limits: chart consumption versus writing/production, nonrandom missing lyrics, classifier domain validity and historical revisions. Relevance: closest long-run design precedent; it offers a competing compensatory-consumption hypothesis, not a justification to select a favorable model. Open article reviewed.

**8. Marco Palomeque and Juan de-Lucio (2024), “The Soundtrack of a Crisis: More Positive Music Preferences During Economic and Social Adversity.”** *Journal of Happiness Studies* 25, article 44; peer reviewed. [Open article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-024-00757-4).
Dataset: Spotify top-200 charts and lyrics across 31 OECD countries around the pandemic, with stream weights. Method: country panel analysis of economic/social adversity and positive music consumption. Reported finding: greater preference for positive music during adversity. Limits: platform selection, country/language comparability, overlapping shocks and much shorter history. Relevance: actual listening weights differ from chart appearances, and composition can change. Reviewing this paper does not authorize choosing a COVID breakpoint or reproducing its campaign.

**9. Alex Edmans, Adrian Fernandez-Perez, Alexandre Garel and Ivan Indriawan (2022), “Music sentiment and stock returns around the world.”** *Journal of Financial Economics* 145, 234–254; peer reviewed. [Author paper](https://alexedmans.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Edmans-et-al.-22-Music-Sentiment.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.08.014).
Dataset: streaming-weighted Spotify acoustic valence and financial markets across 40 countries, 2017–2020. Method: daily/weekly panel associations, reversals and robustness checks. Reported finding: positive contemporaneous return association and subsequent reversal, plus volatility/fund-flow relationships. Limits: acoustic mood proxy, short sample, multiple market outcomes and nonexperimental identification. Relevance: market psychology comparison and a template for documenting return timing, not proof for Billboard lyrics or long historical regimes. Author paper reviewed.

**10. Alicia Weiberg and Jan Heldmann (2026), “Music sentiment and stock returns: A structural break in sentiment dynamics.”** *Finance Research Letters* 102, 110071; peer reviewed. [Publisher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1544612326006008), [author institutional metadata](https://www.fiba.uni-bayreuth.de/de/forschung/publikationen/index.php?y=1).
Dataset: replication of 2017–2020 and extension to 2021–2024 across the same 40-country framework. Method: panel regressions, period interactions and robustness variants. Reported finding: early patterns replicated, but subsequent-week reversal and other relationships did not persist in the extension. Limits: macroeconomic explanation is interpretive; changed proxy quality or timing could also matter. Relevance: directly cautions against extrapolating music–finance findings. Publisher text and institutional authorship verified; no breakpoint imported into our study.

**11. Hal E. Hershfield and Adam L. Alter (2019), “On the naturalistic relationship between mood and entertainment choice.”** *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* 25(3), 458–476; peer reviewed. [Abstract/DOI record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31120264/).
Dataset: archival entertainment choices across music, books, films and cartoons, plus two laboratory experiments. Method: archival associations and boom/recession framing manipulations. Reported finding: economic-context framing changed lighter/heavier entertainment preferences. Limits: laboratory framing is not a macroeconomic natural experiment; outcomes vary across cultural products. Relevance: identifies choice/consumption as a separate mechanism from artists expressing their own hardship. Indexed publisher abstract reviewed; effect-size transfer is not assumed.

**12. Karol Jan Borowiecki (2017), “How Are You, My Dearest Mozart? Well-Being and Creativity of Three Famous Composers Based on Their Letters.”** *Review of Economics and Statistics*; peer reviewed. [Author paper](https://kjb.staff.sdu.dk/pdfs/How%20are%20you%20my%20dearest%20Mozart.%20Borowiecki.%20REStat,%202017.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1162/REST_a_00616).
Dataset: roughly 1,400 letters from three composers, biographical information and creative output. Method: text-derived well-being measures and longitudinal models. Reported finding: negative emotions linked to outstanding creative production. Limits: selected historical individuals, letters rather than chart lyrics, identification depends on the model. Relevance: production mechanisms require artist-level and creation-date evidence absent from chart-month joins. Author article reviewed.

**13. Lindsey A. Larson (2022), “The Lure of Musical Comfort: An Analysis of How Consumer Preferences in Popular Music Change Depending on the State of the U.S. Economy using Billboard Hot 100, Spotify API, & Economic Data.”** Claremont McKenna senior thesis; **not a peer-reviewed journal study**. [Institutional record](https://scholarship.claremont.edu/cmc_theses/3076/).
Dataset: Hot 100 songs, 1958–May 2021, Spotify features and the misery index. Method: twelve feature regressions and a constructed music-misery score. Reported finding: several audio characteristics associated with economic hardship. Limits: constructed score uses fitted coefficients; this is not independent validation, and statistical testing/selection need separate scrutiny. Relevance: an exploratory lead for misery-index comparisons, with a lower evidential status. Thesis abstract and degree metadata reviewed.

These studies motivate a small set of competing, preregistered comparisons. They do not justify searching every source, lag, emotion and genre until a low p-value appears. Suitable future work would preserve the existing classifiers, explicitly choose chart exposure versus debut cohorts, evaluate measurement stability, use time-aware uncertainty and account for the full family of attempted hypotheses. No such tests were run here.
